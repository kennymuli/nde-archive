The Threshold Archive

Nine thousand peopledescribed the same door.

Between 1998 and 2026, strangers with nothing in common wrote down what happened to them at the edge of death. This is what the whole archive says when you read all of it at once — and where the standard account of it turns out to be wrong.

There is a great deal of data here, and several of the figures reward hovering, tracing and clicking. It is all readable on a phone, but it opens up properly on a desktop.

Before Anything Else — What Each Number Rests On

Not every finding here counts the same accounts.

Some questions can be answered by machine across all 9,764 accounts. Others require someone to read a narrative from beginning to end and record what happened in what order. That second kind is slow, so it runs on a random sample. Every chart below says which it is.

Tier one 9,764 every account

Counts, word totals, dates, countries. Anything a machine can total without interpreting a sentence.

each dot is roughly ten accounts

Which archive a finding draws on also varies. Anything built from the questionnaire — demographics, belief, aftereffects, recall — comes from the near-death archive alone, because only it has those fields mapped and only it carries country and age. Anything built from reading narratives covers all three. Every chart says which above it.

A smaller sample widens the error bar; it does not bias the result. The 598 accounts were drawn at random from the 1,497, which were drawn at random from all 9,764. Reading 598 narratives carefully beats pattern-matching 9,764 badly — a lesson this project learned the hard way, and which Part Two describes.

Finding One — The Question Shapes The Answer

There are two archives here, and they disagree.

Everyone who submits an account does two things. They write their story in their own words. Then they answer about fifty questions.

Nobody had compared the two. When you do, the picture of what a near-death experience contains depends entirely on which half you read — and the gap is not small.

What people confirm when asked, versus what they say unprompted
Asked: 5,400 near-death accounts  ·  Unprompted: 500 near-death narratives
Asked directly Volunteered freely

How to read this — each row is one element measured twice. The amber dot is the share who answer yes when the questionnaire asks about it directly. The blue dot is the share who describe it themselves, unprompted, in their own narrative. The line between them is not a range: it is simply the distance between the two measurements. The longer the line, the more the two methods disagree. Hover any row for detail.

Both measurements come from the near-death archive, so the two dots on a row describe the same population. Prompted rates use the questionnaire (n ≈ 5,400 per item); volunteered rates come from 500 of those narratives read individually. Two independent readers agreed on 96% of these judgements. Reading all 1,500 narratives across the three archives would move some rows sharply — “met the dead” runs at 33.2% pooled against 16.8% here — because the after-death archive is about meeting the dead. Holding the archive constant is what makes the two dots comparable.

Read the top of that chart and the near-death experience is a journey: time dissolves, everything is understood, a border is reached. Read the bottom and it is something much simpler — you leave your body and you see a light.

Three quarters of people agree that time stopped when a form asks them. Fewer than one in five mention it when telling their own story.

So which number is true?

Both. They measure different things, and the honest answer is that the gap itself is the result.

A high prompted rate means

When the element is named, people recognise it in what happened to them. That is real information — recognition is not nothing. But a question supplies the word, the concept and the expectation all at once.

A high spontaneous rate means

The element was salient enough to reach for unprompted, in the person's own vocabulary. This is the stronger evidence that it was central to the experience rather than merely acknowledged afterwards.

A wide gap means

Either the element is genuinely present but peripheral — true, yet not what the experience was about — or the question is prompting agreement that would not have arisen on its own. This data cannot separate those two.

A narrow gap means

The element survives both tests. People raise it themselves and confirm it when asked. Only two elements do this: leaving the body, and the light.

The practical consequence is that the familiar description of this experience — tunnel, life review, border, timelessness — is largely built from prompted answers. Read only what people volunteer, and a smaller, stranger core remains.

Finding Two — The Shape Of The Story

The beginning and end are fixed. The middle is a coin flip.

Six hundred accounts were coded event by event, in the order each person told it. Some pairs of events never once appeared in the wrong order.

How reliably one event precedes another, among accounts containing both

What the events mean

The most likely path, assembled from the transitions

No single account contains all of this, and the middle steps are close to unordered. But following the highest-probability transition at each stage produces one canonical route. Solid connectors are near-deterministic; dashed ones are near coin flips.

Assembled by chaining the most frequent next-event at each stage across 598 accounts. It is a modal path, not a template — treat the dashed segments as genuinely unordered rather than as a sequence we are unsure about.

The frame is rigid. Crisis comes first, return comes last, and leaving the body always precedes looking down at it — 119 times out of 119, never once reversed.

But the famous middle sequence — tunnel, then light, then beings — barely holds together. Whether the light comes before arriving somewhere is a 51% call. That is a coin flip. The canonical ordering people recite is, in the middle, mostly something we added afterwards.

Rows are ordered by where each event typically falls, so the diagonal is the canonical sequence rather than an assumption imposed on the data. These 200 accounts take 185 different orderings between them, and 177 of those orderings belong to exactly one account. The commonest single path is shared by seven. Only 39% get from start to finish without doubling back. The shape is real — the threads crowd the diagonal and thin out away from it — but almost nobody walks it cleanly.

Finding Three — Who Is Waiting

Mostly, it is family. Rarely, it is God.

First, how often anyone is there at all.

81.1%of experiencers described encountering at least one being — 485 of 598 accounts read in full, mentioning 951 beings between them. The chart below breaks down who those 951 were.
Who appears, as a share of all 951 beings described
598 accounts — all three archives, coded event by event
Share of 951 encounters

An unidentified presence — someone felt but never named — appears more often than Jesus, God and angels combined. Add deceased parents and grandparents and the picture is domestic, not theological.

What the literature emphasises: wordless, mind-to-mind contact. It is the detail most often singled out as marking these encounters as unlike ordinary life.

What the accounts describe: mostly talking. Of encounters where a mode of communication is described at all, ordinary speech outnumbers telepathy by more than two to one — 34.9% spoken against 14.8% wordless. In a further 39.2%, no exchange is described in either direction; the being is simply present.

14.6% of encounters with the dead were with someone the experiencer says they did not know had died at the time.

Finding Four — The Words People Reach For

The vocabulary is narrower than the experience.

Every narrative was scanned for the words that cluster around the three most recognisable elements. What people describe varies enormously. How they describe it barely varies at all.

Words appearing near “light” and near “tunnel”
5,749 narratives — every near-death account with text
Near “light” Near “tunnel”

Counts of content words within roughly forty words of each term, across all narratives.

The light is white and it is bright — those two words dominate everything else. The third most common neighbour is not a visual word at all: it is love, which appears near “light” 3,417 times, more often than “bright”.

The tunnel is almost never described alone. “Light” sits near it 3,493 times, and the next most common neighbour is “end”. It is written about as a passage toward something rather than as a place in itself — which is consistent with how rarely it appears at all, in one account in ten.

Which religious figures are named
5,749 narratives · 38.4% name at least one
Mentions across the corpus

There is a cliff in that list. God, angels, Jesus and Christ account for almost everything; Buddha, the Virgin Mary, Allah, Krishna and Muhammad together account for 117 mentions against God's 5,357.

Read carefully, this is a fact about who writes in before it is a fact about what anyone saw. The corpus is 61% American and overwhelmingly Anglophone, so the vocabulary available to describe an encounter is overwhelmingly Christian. It says little about whether the underlying experiences differ — and the cross-cultural comparison earlier on this page suggests the sensory elements do not.

A limit worth naming: word proximity is not meaning. “Dark” appearing near “tunnel” 723 times does not establish that the tunnel was dark, only that both words occur close together. These counts describe the language of the corpus, not its content.

Finding Six — The Paradox

The people who say it cannot be described, describe the most.

"There are no words for it" reads like an apology for a thin account. It is the opposite.

Average number of distinct events an account describes
598 accounts — all three archives, coded event by event
Mean events per account

Accounts were sorted into three groups by how often the experiencer states that the experience cannot be put into words. Each bar is the average count of distinct events those accounts go on to describe — separation, light, an encounter, a message, and so on. If “beyond words” meant having little to say, the bars would fall. They rise.

598 accounts read in full. Events counted from a fixed 16-item vocabulary, so the scale is comparable across groups.

People who insist language fails them report 54% more structural content than people who never mention it. Ineffability is not an absence of material. It is a marker of having more to say.

It predicts life change better than any dramatic content does — better than the light, better than the tunnel. Saying "I can't explain it" forecasts transformation at 75.0% against 57.9%. Reaching a border, the most cinematic moment available, predicts it at exactly zero difference.

Finding Seven — What Actually Changes A Life

The drama predicts nothing. The speechlessness predicts everything.

Every experiencer is asked, plainly: did your values and beliefs change because of this? Two thirds say yes. The question is what separates them from the third who say no.

It is not the spectacular content. Compare the most cinematic moment in the whole corpus with the least cinematic thing a person can say.

Share reporting changed values and beliefs afterwards
4,593–4,803 near-death accounts — everyone who answered both items
Reported the element Did not report it

Two elements, each splitting the corpus in two. For each, the amber bar is the share of people who did report that element and later said their values changed; the blue bar is the same share among people who did not. A difference between the pair means the element predicts life change. Identical bars mean it predicts nothing at all.

n = 4,593 for ineffability, 4,803 for the border, among those answering both items.

Reaching a border — the threshold, the point of no return, the single most dramatic beat available — predicts life change at 66.7% against 66.7%. Identical to the decimal. It makes no difference whatsoever.

Saying “I cannot put it into words” predicts it at 75.0% against 57.9% — a seventeen-point gap.

Which inverts the intuition. The part that would make a film — the tunnel, the barrier, the turning back — leaves no measurable mark on how people afterwards describe their own values. The thing that does is the admission that the experience exceeded language. Whatever changes a life here is not the spectacle. It is the encounter with something the person could not fit into words.

Finding Eight — Something Changed

The life review did not fade. It fell off a cliff.

Your whole life flashing before you is the most cinematically famous element of all. It held steady for thirty years — then, in experiences from the 2010s, it halved.

Reported life review, by decade the experience occurred
3,600+ near-death accounts — everyone with a dated experience
Share reporting a life review

Every dated account, grouped by the decade the experience happened in. No filtering by how long anyone waited before writing — see the check below for why that matters.

n shown per decade. Accounts without a stated experience date are excluded.

Flat at 57–61% from the 1980s through the 2000s, then 31.8% for experiences in the 2010s. Not a slow erosion — a step change in a single decade.

The check that nearly produced the opposite story

People write in at wildly different distances from the event. Splitting by that distance is the standard way to test whether a trend is really about memory rather than about the era — but the windows are not available in every decade, and choosing among them can manufacture a trend that is not there.

Reading only the middle two columns would produce a smooth decline from about 70% to about 27% — and it would be wrong. The 1980s have no under-ten-year column, because the archive did not exist yet, while for the 2000s that column holds the largest group and runs the other way at 70.0%. Any trend drawn from a subset of these columns is an artefact of which ones happen to be populated.

Comparing like with like — accounts written within ten years of the event, the only window available for both recent decades — the fall is real and sharp: 70.0% for the 2000s against 29.1% for the 2010s.

Why? Here is what it is not.

Each obvious explanation was tested and each failed. What remains is genuinely open.

Not the question.The same item, with the same answer options, runs across all four decades. The 2010s simply answer “No” far more often.
Not shorter or lazier accounts.2010s narratives are longer — 448 median words against 409 — and their questionnaire answers are longer still, 672 against 547.
Not shallower experiences.By the Greyson scale, the field's own depth measure, 2010s accounts score higher: 14.1 mean against 12.0.
Not only the life review.The same decade shows drops in seeing the future (−30), sudden total understanding (−20), leaving the body (−19) and meeting the dead (−14) — while light, altered time, ineffability and reported life change hold steady or rise.

That last point is the shape of it. What fell away is the elaborated narrative — the review, the vision of the future, the encounter, the total knowledge. What held is the sensory and emotional core — the light, the strangeness of time, the failure of language, the changed life.

One further change is visible and may or may not be the cause: the archive's own editors classified 70% of 2000s accounts as definite near-death experiences, against 55% for the 2010s, with “probable” and “possible” rising correspondingly. Whether that reflects a shift in who submits, in how the archive triages, or in something about the experiences themselves, this data cannot say. It is the single most interesting unresolved question in the corpus.

Finding Nine — Does It Depend On Who You Are

The sensations are universal. The story around them is not.

Split the same twelve elements by gender, then by region, and a clean line appears — one nobody set out to look for.

What the body seems to do is reported at the same rate by everyone. What it is taken to mean is where the groups separate.

Region groups Western Europe, North America, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa against everywhere else. The comparison group is small (roughly 700 accounts) and internally very mixed, so read it as a signal rather than a settled fact.

Near-death archive — 2,400–5,400 per item

“Became more liberal” counts those who reported a non-liberal stance before and a liberal one after.

Where women and men differ

On the structural elements they are indistinguishable. Leaving the body: 56.8% against 57.4% — the one element men report slightly more often. The life review is identical: 55.5% against 55.5%. Entering another world differs by 1.4 points.

The gaps open only on interpretation and aftermath. Women report abilities afterwards at 46.2% against 37.3% — the largest demographic gap anywhere in the corpus. They report the experience as beyond words more often (53.3% against 46.8%), and reaching a border more often (69.1% against 63.3%). Same reported events; different account of what they meant and what followed.

Where cultures differ

The elements usually called culturally Western turn out not to be. The tunnel is reported more often outside the West — 36.4% against 29.7% — and so is the light, 64.4% against 59.8%. Time distortion, entering another realm and ineffability show no meaningful difference at all.

What does differ is relational and narrative: meeting the dead (45.7% vs 37.3%), the life review (56.5% vs 50.4%), and glimpsing the future (44.6% vs 38.5%) are all more common in Western accounts. The raw sensory core travels; the biography wrapped around it does not.

Read this one with the most caution of anything on this page. These archives are English-language and American-hosted: 61% of accounts that name a country name the United States, and the comparison group here is roughly 630 accounts from everywhere else combined — Mexico, India, Brazil, the Philippines and some sixty other countries pooled into a single bucket because no one of them is large enough to stand alone. That bucket is not “non-Western culture”. It is “people who found an American website and wrote to it in English”. The differences above clear significance, but what separates the groups may be who reaches the archive rather than what happens at the threshold.

Finding Eleven — What Happens To Belief

Most people keep their religion. Almost nobody keeps their certainty.

Experiencers state their religious position twice — as it was before, and as it is now. The two answers turn out to measure different things, and only one of them moves.

The item records two separate facts: a stance — conservative, moderate or liberal — and an affiliation, the tradition itself. Separated, they behave nothing alike.

Religious stance, before and after
2,395 near-death accounts stating a stance both times

Each row is where people started. The three figures are where they ended up. Read across a row to see how a group dispersed. Movement is not symmetrical: liberal is the hardest position to leave and the easiest to arrive at.

Net effect: liberal rises from 35.1% to 42.1% of the corpus; conservative falls slightly, from 20.5% to 19.0%. 73.4% report no change at all.

Who keeps the tradition they arrived with
3,851 near-death accounts naming an affiliation both times
Share reporting the same affiliation afterwards

Affiliation is stickier than stance: 62.2% name the same tradition afterwards. But the retention rates are not evenly spread, and the lowest one is the one worth noticing.

Of experiencers who described themselves as atheist beforehand, 36.9% still did afterwards — the lowest retention of any group, well below Catholics (57.8%) or Protestants (58.0%). The position least likely to survive the experience is the one that held there was nothing to survive it.

A caution on this one: prior affiliation is self-reported afterwards, in the same sitting as the current answer. Someone recalling their earlier position through the lens of what happened may not recall it as they would have stated it at the time. That applies to every before-and-after item here.

Finding Twelve — Belief Before The Experience

Prior belief shifts the odds. It does not decide them.

Everyone states their religion as it was before. Set that against whether a religious figure is named anywhere in the account, and the effect is real — but so is what happens to the people who had no belief at all.

Accounts naming a religious figure, by religion held beforehand
3,187 near-death accounts stating a prior religion
Names God, Jesus, an angel or another figure

Affiliated beforehand: 42.3%. Unaffiliated: 30.7%. An eleven-point gap, and statistically unambiguous at this sample size.

28.4% of people who described themselves as atheist before the experience nonetheless name a religious figure in their account — most often God.

So belief predicts the rate without gating it. Nearly three in ten prior atheists reach for this vocabulary anyway, against roughly four in ten Catholics. The difference between a lifelong believer and a self-described atheist is fourteen points — smaller than most people would guess, in either direction.

The figure named barely varies. God leads for every group including atheists and the unaffiliated; Jesus and angels follow in the same order throughout. What prior belief changes is how often the vocabulary appears, not which vocabulary is available.

Worth stating: prior religion is reported after the fact, in the same sitting as the account itself. Someone whose beliefs changed may not describe their earlier position as they would have described it at the time.

Finding Thirteen — What Forty Years Does To It

Memory takes less than you would expect, and takes it selectively.

Accounts arrive anywhere from months to half a century after the event. If these were ordinary memories decaying, everything should thin with the gap. Almost nothing does.

Element rate by how long the writer waited
3,924 near-death accounts with both dates recorded
Under 10 years Over 40 years

Bands: under 10 years (n=1,433), 10–25 (907), 25–40 (869), over 40 (715). The two extremes are shown; the middle bands sit between them.

The light does not fade: 65.8% at under ten years, 68.3% at over forty. Nor does the sense that words fail, nor reported life change. Across four decades of delay these barely move.

What does thin is the interpretive layer. Sudden total understanding falls from a peak of 73.8% to 60.6%. Meeting the dead declines steadily, 48.4% to 38.4%. And past forty years the writing itself contracts — narratives shorten by around 13% and carry fewer distinct elements.

This is the same shape as the decade finding, arrived at from a completely different direction. Whether you sort by when the experience happened or by how long someone waited to write, the sensory core holds and the elaborated narrative erodes. Two independent cuts of the data, one pattern.

+18.0 The single largest movement runs the other way. Claimed abilities afterwards rise from 40.4% to 58.4% with delay — not memory fading but a longer window in which to notice something and attribute it.

Finding Fourteen — What "Abilities Afterwards" Actually Means

Half of them say something changed. Almost none of them tested it.

Around half of experiencers answer yes to being asked whether they have psychic or other unusual capacities they lacked before. It is the largest aftereffect in the corpus after changed values — and the least examined. So here is what the 2,159 people who said yes and then explained actually describe.

What the reported abilities are
2,159 near-death answers with a written explanation
Share of those answering yes

Categories overlap — 15% name more than one. Half the answers fit none of them; the median explanation is 26 words, often too brief to classify.

The two commonest are quieter than the word “psychic” suggests. Knowing things without being told (18.9%) and sensing or seeing the dead (17.9%) lead. Foreknowledge of events follows at 9.3%, healing at 7.1%, and picking up other people's feelings or illness at 6.3%.

Further down are the more cinematic claims: hearing guidance (3.7%), seeing auras (2.5%), leaving the body at will (2.4%), and interfering with electrical devices (1.5%). The last of these is a fixture of popular accounts and is among the rarest things anyone actually reports.

Is any of it tested?

3.6% of these answers mention testing, verification or confirmation of any kind — and that count includes informal confirmation by a friend or relative, not formal assessment.

So the honest answer is no. Nothing in this archive constitutes a test. The questionnaire asks people whether they have such abilities; it does not ask them to demonstrate one, and no mechanism here could evaluate a demonstration if offered. These are self-reports, collected once, at a distance of years.

What the corpus does show is that 6.9% hedge or actively doubt their own claim in the same breath as making it — attributing it to coincidence, or saying they are unsure whether it counts. That is a small share, but it is not zero, and it is the only internal check the data contains.

Two limits on this section. Half the answers matched none of my categories, so the breakdown describes the classifiable half rather than the whole. And the categories were written after reading a sample, which means they fit this corpus by construction — a different reader would draw different lines. Treat the ranking as sturdier than the exact percentages.

Finding Fifteen — The Part That Gets Left Out

Not all of it is light.

Popular accounts of this subject are overwhelmingly benign — peace, love, reunion. The archive is not.

Frightening experiences are harder to submit and harder to publish, so any archive of volunteered accounts will under-represent them. Even so, they are plainly present, and leaving them out would misdescribe the corpus.

Fear and darkness, by archive
1,497 accounts read individually
Fear or terror described Darkness or void described

Peace shown alongside for scale. An account can contain both — many do.

15.9% of accounts describe fear or terror with no peace and no love reported anywhere in them — roughly one in six.

That is the closest this data comes to identifying a distressing experience: not merely frightening at the outset, which is common, but frightening without the resolution that usually follows.

Explicitly infernal language is rarer but not marginal. 16.4% of narratives use it somewhere — “hell” appears in 620 accounts, “screaming” in 583, “evil” in 308, with the devil, Satan, demons and the abyss trailing well behind. Out-of-body and related experiences carry by far the most fear (39.0%), roughly double the near-death rate.

Frightening accounts are easy to leave out of a summary — they are less quotable, less shareable, and less like the story most people expect. Omitting them would give a rosier picture than the archive supports, which is why they have a section of their own.

Finding Sixteen — Things That Could Have Been Checked

More than one in four, where the experience is about someone else.

A recurring shape in these accounts: the experiencer learns something they say they had no way of knowing, and someone else later tells them it was right.

Every such claim was recorded — what was asserted, how specific it was, and whether anyone is said to have confirmed it. Nothing here is adjudicated. The question is what people claim and in what detail, not whether it happened. The after-death communication archive has now been read in full: 1,588 accounts, every one coded claim by claim.

Accounts asserting a specific claim confirmed by someone else
1,987 accounts — the ADC archive in full, plus samples of the others
Share of accounts

“Specific” excludes vague impressions. “Confirmed” means the account states that another person verified it.

Across the after-death archive alone, 2,348 claims were recorded. 920 assert third-party confirmation and 581 of those are specific rather than impressionistic. They appear in 440 of 1,588 accounts — 27.7%, with a margin of just ±2.2 points.

The concentration is the finding. After-death accounts carry these at 27.7% against 12.5% for near-death and 9.5% for out-of-body experiences. The claims cluster where the experience is about someone else rather than about the experiencer.

±2.2 Reading a 199-account sample of this archive gave 26.1%. Reading all 1,588 gives 27.7% — a shift of 1.6 points, with the margin cut from ±6.1 to ±2.2. Sampling was tested against the full count here and held, which is the reason to trust the sampled figures elsewhere on this page.

What is actually being claimed

The largest category is now one that did not exist in the first pass. Learning a specific fact the experiencer says they had no way of knowing accounts for 214 of the 581 confirmed claims — more than any other kind. In the earlier coding these were disappearing into a generic “other” bucket; naming the category pulled them out.

Of 542 such claims in total, 259 state that another person later confirmed the fact. And the sharpest version remains the smallest: 77 accounts describe meeting someone the experiencer says they did not know had died, 66 of them asserting the death was later confirmed. Across the full archive, 14.4% of all encounters with the dead are with someone not known to have died — almost exactly the 14.6% the small sample predicted.

Two limits, unchanged by the larger corpus. A third of confirmed claims still fall into “other”, so the category breakdown remains partly provisional even after adding a code for the commonest pattern. And every one of these is a recollection written down later, in which the sequence of learning and confirming is reported by the same person. Nothing in a corpus of volunteered accounts can establish what anyone knew and when. What it can establish is that the pattern is not rare, not confined to one culture, and not going away.

If You Remember Only This

Fourteen things the archive says.

Every one of these is a count, not an interpretation. None of them says anything about what caused these experiences.

  1. The question shapes the answer

    Ask directly about time distortion and 76.6% agree. Let people simply tell their story and 17.6% mention it. The familiar description of a near-death experience may be, in part, a description of the questionnaire that collected it.

  2. The frame is rigid, the middle is improvised

    Crisis first, return last — holding in 98–100% of accounts containing both. But whether the light comes before or after arriving somewhere is 51%. A coin flip.

  3. It happens in the bedroom

    Bedroom 25.8%, hospital room 19.9%, tunnel 10.4%, garden 3.2%. Most of this is reported as happening exactly where the person's body already was.

  4. Mostly it is family, rarely God

    81.1% meet someone. An unidentified presence appears more often than Jesus, God and angels combined, and ordinary speech outnumbers wordless communication more than two to one.

  5. Speechlessness predicts a changed life; drama predicts nothing

    Saying it cannot be described forecasts changed values at 75.0% against 57.9%. Reaching a border — the most cinematic beat available — predicts it at 66.7% against 66.7%. Identical.

  6. The sensations travel; the story does not

    Tunnel, light, time distortion and ineffability show no meaningful East–West difference. Meeting the dead, the life review and glimpsing the future are all markedly more Western.

  7. Men and women report the same events, a different aftermath

    Indistinguishable on leaving the body and the life review. Then a 12.7-point gap on abilities reported afterwards — the largest demographic difference in the corpus.

  8. Belief moves in one direction only

    73.4% keep the religious stance they arrived with. But of those who move, the traffic runs one way — liberal is the hardest position to leave and the easiest to reach. The lowest retention of any group is atheist, at 36.9%.

  9. What fades is the story, not the sensation

    Across four decades of delay before writing, the light holds at 65.8% then 68.3%, and so do ineffability and reported life change. Sudden total understanding falls 73.8% to 60.6%, meeting the dead 48.4% to 38.4%. Sort by decade or by delay and the same layer erodes.

  10. Prior belief shifts the odds, it does not set them

    The affiliated name a religious figure in 42.3% of accounts, the unaffiliated in 30.7%. But 28.4% of prior atheists name one too — most often God, the same figure that leads for every group.

  11. Half report new abilities. Almost none were tested

    The commonest are quiet ones: knowing things unprompted 18.9%, sensing the dead 17.9%. Electrical interference, a fixture of popular accounts, is among the rarest at 1.5%. Just 3.6% mention verification of any kind.

  12. One account in six is frightening and stays that way

    15.9% describe fear or terror with no peace and no love anywhere in them. Out-of-body accounts carry the most at 39.0%, roughly double the near-death rate. The benign version of this subject is a selection effect.

  13. The claims cluster where the experience is about someone else

    27.7% of after-death accounts assert something specific that another person later confirmed — against 12.5% for near-death and 9.5% for out-of-body. Read in full across all 1,588.

  14. The life review fell off a cliff

    Steady at 57–61% from the 1980s through the 2000s, then 31.8% for experiences in the 2010s. Comparing only accounts written within ten years, the drop is 70.0% to 29.1% in a single decade.

And What It Might Mean

What follows from this, and what does not.

Nine thousand people sat down and wrote out the most significant thing that ever happened to them, for strangers, without being asked to and without anything in return. Whatever else this archive is, it is that first.

What follows is what emerges when all of it is read at once. Counting cannot settle what these experiences were — that was never in its power. What it can do is show where the accounts agree with each other, and where the familiar version of this story does not match what the people who lived it actually wrote.

If you are trying to make sense of your own life

The dramatic part is not the part that changes people.Reaching a border predicts changed values at 66.7% against 66.7% — no effect whatsoever. Being unable to put the experience into words predicts it at 75.0% against 57.9%. Whatever transforms a life here is not the spectacle.
The setting is ordinary.A quarter of these accounts are set in a bedroom, a fifth in a hospital room. Whatever people describe, most of it is reported as happening exactly where they already were.
Two thirds report changed values afterwards.That is the most consistent aftereffect in the corpus — steadier than any single element of the experience itself, and stable across gender, region and decade.
What changes is not what you would brace for.Sorting by how long people waited before writing, the sensory core holds across four decades while the interpretive layer thins. Whatever these experiences are, the part that persists in memory is the sensation, not the explanation built around it.
Certainty moves; affiliation mostly does not.73.4% keep their religious stance and 62.2% keep their tradition. Where movement happens it runs one way, toward the more liberal position, and the lowest retention of any group is atheist at 36.9%.

If you are thinking about someone you have lost

The people who appear are overwhelmingly family.Deceased parents and grandparents together outnumber every named religious figure combined. An unnamed presence — someone felt but never identified — is the single most common category of all.
Encounters are described as ordinary in manner.Where a mode of contact is described, plain speech outnumbers wordless communication two to one. In four cases in ten nothing is exchanged at all; the person is simply present.
Roughly half report something new about themselves afterwards.Most often it is quiet — knowing things without being told, or a sense of the person they lost. Worth saying plainly: nothing in this archive tests any of it, and only 3.6% of those reporting an ability mention verification of any kind. It is what people say about their own lives, recorded once, years later.
One detail stands out.14.6% of encounters with the dead were with someone the experiencer says they did not know had died at the time. It recurs across decades, countries and languages, in accounts written by people with no contact with one another. What to make of it is beyond anything counting can settle — but it is there, consistently, and it is not a small number.

Where the counting stops

Every limit here is a limit of arithmetic, not of the accounts. Percentages describe a corpus, not a person: no figure on this page tells any individual what their own experience was, and a rare element is not a less real one. The people who wrote these did not submit data — they described something that changed their lives, and the parts that mattered most to them are frequently the parts that no coding scheme can hold.

Two things follow. Because everyone here chose to write to an archive about near-death experience, none of these rates estimate how common any of this is in the wider world; they describe who wrote in. And where the accounts contradict the popular picture — the ordinary rooms, the plain conversation, the middle of the sequence that turns out to have no fixed order — the thing being corrected is the popular picture, not the people. On every point where the familiar story and the accounts disagree, this page sides with the accounts.

What no method reaches is the thing most of them were actually trying to say. The strongest statistical signal in the entire corpus is that the people who insist their experience cannot be put into words are the ones it changed most. That is as close as counting gets to admitting what it is missing.

Why I Built This

Two fears, and what reading nine thousand accounts did to one of them.

I started reading these for reasons that have nothing to do with statistics, and it seems right to say so plainly rather than let the charts imply I came to this neutrally.

I have two fears. One is losing the people I love. The other is being the one who is lost — becoming, someday, an absence in someone else's life.

Reading these accounts has quieted the first. Not settled it, and not by proving anything. Something about sitting with thousands of people who describe the same threshold, in their own words, from every country and every decade, has made that fear smaller than it was.

The second one I cannot read my way out of. But I hope that what calmed me might reach the people I love — my child especially. That when I am gone, what they carry is not only that I am gone for good, but some part of the hope these strangers were willing to write down.

Where I actually stand

I am agnostic, in the specific sense that I believe there is something beyond us — and that I cannot be argued into a position on what it is, or on whether it has any interest in this life.

Reading these accounts has not resolved that. What it has done is make me hold the first half more firmly than before: that there is a beyond, and that what these thousands of people describe finding there is, overwhelmingly, connection and love.

But the reason I keep going is not to shore that up. It is the opposite. I would rather test the belief than protect it — approach it as a hypothesis, look at what the accounts actually say instead of what I would like them to say, and get close enough to something like a real conclusion that it could turn out I was wrong.

That is the motivation, and it is worth knowing when you read the numbers. It is also why every finding here is reported as it came out, including the ones that cut against a comforting reading — the frightening accounts, the elements that turn out rarer than the story suggests, the abilities almost nobody has tested, and the plain fact that none of this establishes what any of it means.

What I have come to believe from this research

Three things settled in me over the course of it. None of them is a finding. Everything above this section is what the accounts say and can be checked against the data. What follows is only what reading them did to one person, and it should be weighed accordingly.

There is something beyond this, and I do not know what it is. I want to be precise about how little that claims — not a place, not a mechanism, not a promise about who is waiting. Only that the idea of everything simply stopping here has come to seem to me the least likely account on offer. I thought that before this research, which is exactly why I will not present this research as having proved it.

People are good. This is the one I did not expect to change. The internet had me fairly well convinced otherwise. But the scene I kept meeting was this: someone arrives in front of a person they had lost, carrying something they never got to say — an apology, usually, or some version of one — and is told, in one form or another, that it was already alright, and to go back and make something of the time they have left. That is not universal, and this page does not pretend it is; the frightening accounts are documented a few sections above and I have not looked away from them. But forgiveness is the ordinary case in what I read, not the rare one. I have come to think that our capacity to understand each other, to feel with each other, and to forgive is the native condition rather than the achievement.

Love may be the only real thing. This is the hardest to say without sounding like a greeting card, so let me say it plainly. Ask people what the light was, or the being, or the place, and what comes back is very often not a description at all. It is love — at an intensity they say has no equivalent here and no words able to carry it. It is what people reach for after every other word has failed them, which is the part I cannot get past.

What I have come to think is that everything else we feel is a fragment of it. Jealousy is a fear about the absence of being loved. Grief is love with nowhere left to go. Put a white light through a prism and it comes apart into colours, and we spend our lives naming the colours and mistaking them for separate things — when what we are looking at each time is one light, split by whatever it happened to pass through.

I hold all three loosely, and none of them follows from the numbers. A corpus of volunteered accounts can tell you what people report; it cannot tell you what is true beyond the reporting. If you finish this page believing something different, that is an entirely reasonable place to land — and I would genuinely like to hear it.

Part Two

Methodology

Everything above is a claim about tens of thousands of pages. Here is how those pages were counted, what was checked, and the five occasions the numbers were confidently wrong before they were right.

What Nearly Went Wrong

Five times the data lied convincingly.

Every number above survived a specific attempt to break it. These are the attempts that succeeded, and what they would have produced if nobody had checked.

01

Answers with elaboration were being dropped

Many people write "Yes" and then keep going. Counting only the bare word discarded every one of them.

Saw a light: read as 9.7% → actually 62.6%
02

Six questions were never yes/no at all

They offer descriptive alternatives instead, so the word "yes" appears in no valid answer.

Time distortion: read as 0.0% → actually 76.6%
03

Pattern-matching missed most of what it looked for

Automated text matching found only 40% of what human readers found, and its accuracy varied wildly by element — so the ranking between elements was arbitrary.

Retired and replaced by 1,500 read-through judgements
04

An age pattern was really a waiting-time pattern

Reports of psychic ability appeared to fall with age at the time of the experience. They actually rose with how long the person had to notice.

Apparent 34-point age gradient → mostly recall interval
05

Pooling three archives invented a pattern

Combining collections with different base rates made encounters with the dead appear to repel every other element. Within a single collection, they are among the strongest pairings.

Simpson's paradox, caught by splitting the corpora

None of these threw an error. Each produced a confident, plausible, publishable number. All five were caught the same way — by a figure being implausible rather than by anything failing.

Still provisional: the claim-type taxonomy, where a third of cases fall into an "other" bucket; the message themes, which are keyword matches never validated against blind reading; and the life-review measure, whose two affirmative options behave so differently that merging them was probably a mistake. These are flagged rather than fixed.

Everything here is descriptive. It reports what tens of thousands of pages say and how often they say it. It makes no claim about what produced the experiences.
